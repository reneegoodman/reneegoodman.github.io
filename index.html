About Me

I am a physicist by training with an interest in interdisciplinary problems. I completed a BSc in Biology and Physics from the University of Windsor in 2021 where I conducted research with the Rondeau-Gagné group on conjugated polymers for flexible electronics. Following my bachelor's degree I had a brief interlude as a physicist for Thermtest Inc. where I worked on developing instruments for measuring the thermal properties of material. In 2023 I started my MSc in experimental condensend matter physics at McGill University with Prof. Guillaume Gervais. My thesis on Mass Flow and Noise Measurements in a Nanoelectrofluidic Device was completed in 2025. I have now switched to theoretical physics for my PhD, under the supervision of Prof. Viola Priesemann at the Max Planck Institute for Dynamics and Self-Organization, where I use tools from statistical physics and network theory to analyze large connectomics datasets, such as the Female Adult Fly Brain to understand how brain networks are organized, and how they process information.

In my spare time I love to lift weights, go bikepacking, knit, and care for my many houseplants.

Mass Flow and Noise Measurements in a Nanoelectrofluidic Device

Classical mass transport experiments on the nanoscale examine the flow of gases through nano- channels or nano-pores to determine parameters such as transmission probability, conductance, and geometry. These parameters are critical for understanding the behaviour of molecules in these confined domains. At high pressures, a particular geometry of pore called the de Laval nozzle can accelerate gases to supersonic speeds and generate sonic black holes, a process first theorized by W. G. Unruh in 1976. During my MSc thesis I experimentally measured the flow of He4 gas through an 80nm de Laval nozzle and achieved super-sonic flow rates. This was an important proof-of-principle experiment for measuring the Unruh effect.
